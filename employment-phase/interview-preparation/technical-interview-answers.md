Technical Interview Answers

1. What is GRC?

Short Answer:
GRC stands for Governance, Risk and Compliance. It helps organizations establish direction, identify and manage risks, and meet applicable legal, regulatory, and internal requirements.

Practical Example:
An organization identifies access-control risks, establishes an access policy, implements controls, and checks whether the controls meet relevant requirements.

Relevant Portfolio Evidence:
ISO 27001, Enterprise Risk Management, and UAE NCA ECC projects.

Follow-up Question:
How do governance and risk management work together?

---

2. What is the difference between governance, risk, and compliance?

Short Answer:
Governance establishes direction and accountability. Risk management identifies and treats uncertainty that may affect objectives. Compliance checks whether applicable requirements are being met.

Practical Example:
Management approves an information-security policy, the risk team assesses threats to the organization, and compliance staff map controls to relevant requirements.

Relevant Portfolio Evidence:
ISO 27001 and NCA ECC educational simulations.

Follow-up Question:
Can an organization be compliant but still have security risks?

Practical Example:
A fictional risk register can record the risk of unauthorized access, assign a risk owner, assess likelihood and impact, and document treatment actions.

Relevant Portfolio Evidence:

4. What is the difference between a threat, vulnerability, risk, and control?

Short Answer:
A threat is a potential cause of harm. A vulnerability is a weakness. Risk represents the possible effect of uncertainty on objectives. A control is a measure used to prevent, detect, or reduce risk.

Practical Example:
A phishing attacker is a threat, weak user awareness is a vulnerability, account compromise is a risk, and security awareness training is a control.

Relevant Portfolio Evidence:
Risk assessment and control documentation.

Follow-up Question:
Can one control address multiple risks?

10. What is the difference between a gap assessment and an audit?

Short Answer:
A gap assessment compares the current state against defined requirements to identify areas for improvement. An audit is a systematic evaluation against criteria that follows an established audit process and scope.

Practical Example:
A portfolio gap assessment identifies missing documentation in a fictional ISMS. It should not be described as an independent certification audit.

13. What is inherent risk versus residual risk?

Short Answer:
Inherent risk is the risk before considering the effect of controls. Residual risk is the remaining risk after controls and treatments are considered.

Practical Example:
A system may have significant inherent access risk, but access reviews and stronger authentication may reduce its residual risk.

18. What should you do if evidence is missing?

Short Answer:
I would confirm the requirement, check whether the evidence exists elsewhere, document the evidence request, and escalate or record the gap according to the process. I would not assume that the control is effective without sufficient support.

Practical Example:
For a missing access-review record, I would contact the responsible owner and document the outcome.

Relevant Portfolio Evidence:
Evidence organization and control-testing exercises.

40. How would you explain a high-risk finding to management?
Short Answer: I would explain the affected asset or process, the risk scenario, potential business impact, supporting evidence, current controls, recommended treatment, and the decision or action required.
Practical Example: For a high-rated access risk, I would focus on the business consequence and the evidence supporting the rating rather than using unexplained technical terminology.
Relevant Portfolio Evidence: Risk register and control-testing projects.
Follow-up Question: How would you communicate uncertainty in the finding?

41. What is the difference between a policy and a procedure?
Short Answer: A policy states management's direction and expectations. A procedure describes the steps used to perform a particular activity.
Practical Example: An access-control policy defines requirements, while an access-review procedure describes how reviewers perform and document the review.
Relevant Portfolio Evidence: Policy and compliance documentation.
Follow-up Question: Who should approve an information-security policy?

42. What is the difference between preventive and detective controls?
Short Answer: Preventive controls aim to stop an unwanted event before it occurs. Detective controls aim to identify an event or issue after or while it occurs.
Practical Example: Access restrictions can be preventive, while monitoring alerts can be detective.
Relevant Portfolio Evidence: Control documentation and NCA ECC mapping.
Follow-up Question: What is the purpose of a corrective control?

43. What is control design effectiveness?
Short Answer: Design effectiveness considers whether a control is appropriately designed to address the relevant risk or requirement.
Practical Example: A policy requiring periodic access reviews may be appropriately designed, but additional testing is needed to determine whether reviews actually occur.
Relevant Portfolio Evidence: Control-testing exercises.
Follow-up Question: Can a well-designed control still fail operationally?

44. What is operating effectiveness?
Short Answer: Operating effectiveness considers whether a control operates as intended over the relevant period and whether sufficient evidence supports that conclusion.
Practical Example: An access review may be properly designed but not performed consistently.
Relevant Portfolio Evidence: Control-testing project.
Follow-up Question: What evidence would you request to test operation?

45. What would you do if evidence contradicts a control owner's statement?
Short Answer: I would remain objective, preserve the relevant evidence, clarify the discrepancy with the control owner, and escalate or document the issue according to the testing process.
Practical Example: If a control owner states that reviews are complete but the records show missing review periods, I would investigate the difference rather than immediately reaching an unsupported conclusion.
Relevant Portfolio Evidence: Evidence-validation exercises.
Follow-up Question: How would you document the final result?

46. What is data minimization?
Short Answer: Data minimization means limiting personal-data collection and processing to what is relevant and necessary for the specified purpose, subject to applicable requirements.
Practical Example: A process should not collect unnecessary personal information simply because it is technically possible.
Relevant Portfolio Evidence: PDPL educational study.
Follow-up Question: Who should determine whether collected data is necessary?

47. What is the difference between a data controller and processor?
Short Answer: The exact legal definitions depend on the applicable law. Generally, a controller determines relevant purposes and means of processing, while a processor processes personal data on behalf of another party under defined instructions.
Practical Example: A business may determine why customer information is processed, while an external service provider processes the information to deliver a contracted service.
Relevant Portfolio Evidence: Data privacy study.
Follow-up Question: Why should contracts define privacy responsibilities?

48. How should vendor risks be prioritized?
Short Answer: Vendor risk can be prioritized using factors such as data sensitivity, business criticality, service dependency, access privileges, regulatory impact, and the vendor's control environment.
Practical Example: A vendor handling sensitive data and supporting a critical service may require more extensive assessment and monitoring.
Relevant Portfolio Evidence: Vendor risk educational simulation.
Follow-up Question: Should every vendor receive the same assessment?
